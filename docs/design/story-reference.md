## Scope and authority

This document expands the story for writers and level designers. The story summary supplies detailed scene and log ideas; the synopsis fixes several newer story facts. Where the summary conflicts with the decisions brief on her death, Earth's fate, or progression order, this reference follows the brief: her death is accidental while he restrains her, records show Earth devastated without proving every person dead, and reveal order does not impose a movement lock. (decisions brief, rulings 1, 2, 8; story summary §§26, 30, 38)

The terms **Tewa** and **Earth** refer to the protagonist's homeworld. **Ko Lu Ki Wena**, rendered “Our Planet,” is the crew civilization's homeworld. The synopsis calls the project Naleya while using the Nalesha working language reference; final naming remains open. (synopsis, opening notes and passage 05; decisions brief, ruling 13)

## Peoples, ecology, and the war

The protagonist comes from Tewa, Earth. The ship and crew come from Ko Lu Ki Wena, “Our Planet.” The two peoples look strikingly alike, but the sources do not explain their biological similarity or fix each world's political naming beyond these terms. “Alien,” “enemy,” and “origin civilization” shift with viewpoint; the protagonist's people are the ones the crew call the enemy. (synopsis, passages 05–06; story summary §§15–16)

Earth's biosphere is collapsing irreversibly. Earth retains water, oxygen, energy, industry, technology, and spacecraft, yet lacks the living ecological stock and biological infrastructure needed to rebuild planetary systems quickly enough. Compatible microbes, starter ecologies, seeds, reproductive stock, cell lines, and bioreactor output are examples of possible aid, not a finalized cargo list. Without outside support, probable extinction lies roughly a century away, preceded by food failure, migration, disease, failing states, violence, and political radicalization. (story summary §16; reconciliation §1.1)

The crew civilization could provide aid, but doing so at the requested scale would require major industrial diversion and a long decline in consumption and living standards. Rationing and population controls appear as possible costs, not established policies. The aid refusal therefore reflects a choice to protect the donor society's standard of life, rather than an inability to help. (story summary §16)

The refusal is followed by a desperate attack from Earth, which loses. As ecological collapse continues, an authoritarian, nationalist survivalist leader centralizes power, suppresses domestic opposition, redirects industry toward war, and plans further attacks. The protagonist dislikes this leader and acts to protect people, not to endorse Earth's government. (story summary §18)

The crew society's hard-line argument draws on real facts about Earth's inequality, lavish cloud or orbital residences, waste, military spending, and corruption. One representative log condemns the whole population and argues that they have earned annihilation. It is a political speaker's opinion, not proof that ordinary people deserve extermination. The society's leaders also turn away refugees and expand security powers, while the captain notices dissent recast as disloyalty. Its relatively egalitarian past does not prevent its current exclusionary turn. (story summary §§17–19)

Infiltration is real. Earth has spies, saboteurs, agents, and sleeper assets, and the protagonist is one. The crew society's response is also indiscriminate: innocent people face suspicion, refugees are treated as potential agents, and security authority expands. The story holds both facts together without making either population uniformly virtuous or wicked. (story summary §18)

## Characters and knowledge

### Protagonist

The protagonist is an Earth-born infiltrator with autobiographical amnesia after the ship's disaster. He retains technical skill, procedural memory, emergency knowledge, and fragments of familiarity with the vessel. His name, formal ship role, cover identity, recruitment route, and detailed Earth affiliation remain open. (story summary §§3, 21; synopsis passages 05, 08)

He initially befriends the captain as part of the infiltration mission. The captain introduces him to his daughter. His love for her and affection for the captain become genuine. By the crisis he is divided between the people of his birth world and the people he has come to love aboard ship. (story summary §21)

### Captain

The captain is a career officer who trusts the protagonist as a close friend and brings him into his family circle. He initially believes the public account of the mission. When the sealed orders unlock near the target, he judges the extermination order wrong and resolves to refuse it. Whether he formally transmits a refusal remains undecided. (story summary §§22, 27; synopsis passages 06–07; decisions brief, ruling 5)

After his daughter's death, he concludes that Command was right and carries out the extermination order. The story does not settle the mechanism, contingency, or timing, including its relation to evacuation. He cannot kill the protagonist, knocks him unconscious, and has him placed in stasis. The counterfactual of whether Earth would have been spared absent the sabotage is unknowable. (synopsis passages 15–16; decisions brief, rulings 5–6)

### Captain's daughter

She is the protagonist's committed mate, the captain's daughter, and pregnant with the protagonist's child. She believes the public mission account and was never told the real orders. Her compassionate politics, affection for animals, loyalty to family, stubbornness, and comfort with military culture make her a full person rather than an omniscient moral voice. Her exact service position and name remain open. (story summary §§20, 25; synopsis passages 08–09; society handoff §11)

She goes to astrometrics to surprise the protagonist with her pregnancy. She is not hunting a saboteur. There she finds the removed S-drive component and recognizes the protagonist's role from that fact and the earlier sabotage. (story summary §25; synopsis passages 12–13)

The protagonist tries to restrain her so she cannot report him. She dies accidentally during the confrontation. The death is not intended, and it is his doing: he blocks her escape and introduces force into the situation. The exact choreography, including weapon handling, remains open. (decisions brief, ruling 1; synopsis passage 14; story summary §26)

## Public mission, sealed orders, and weapons

The public mission is a peace and enforcement operation: diplomacy, an aid package, and a demonstration of overwhelming force intended to compel settlement and stop attacks. The crew, including the captain, believes this account. The actual extermination orders remain sealed and inaccessible to the captain until the ship nears its target. (story summary §22; decisions brief, ruling 7)

The plan has three tiers:

1. A primary atmospheric chain reaction or catastrophic event intended to destroy Earth's biosphere. Its exact weapon form is undecided.
2. A highly contagious, fatal biological agent aimed at Earth's people. The peoples' biological similarity means failed containment would threaten the crew as well. This is a mission contingency, not a present-day infection plot. (story summary §22; reconciliation §1.1)
3. A final contingency that initiates a large S-fold inside Earth's atmosphere. The unprecedentedly large S-drive makes the ship itself a weapon. The physical scale and exact effect remain unspecified. (story summary §§22–23)

The **S-drive** creates folded-space geometry. The **H-drive** travels efficiently through that fold. H-drive alone cannot provide useful interstellar travel. Conventional propulsion can still move a ship while the S-drive is active, though much more slowly. Earlier atmospheric S-drive incidents, including emergency activations to avoid crashes, led to a ban on operational S-drives aboard atmosphere-capable ships. The present vessel exceeds previous drive scale. (story summary §23)

The journey takes about five years with S-drive plus H-drive, and about thirty Earth years with S-drive plus conventional propulsion. Without the S-drive, the trip is not survivable on a human timescale. These are approximate story estimates; exact distance and dates remain open. (decisions brief, ruling 3; synopsis passages 20–21; story summary §23)

## Sabotage, death, and reversal

The protagonist's sabotage has two stages. Earlier in the voyage, he damages the H-drive to delay the ship as part of his original infiltration mission. The action comes too late to prevent the ship reaching dangerous proximity to Earth; S-drive plus conventional propulsion can continue the trip. (story summary §24; synopsis passage 10)

Near the target, the sealed orders unlock. The protagonist learns that the S-drive is a weapon even if the primary weapon is disabled. He removes a critical portable S-drive component and takes it to astrometrics, where he hides it. The component's exact name and why astrometrics is needed remain open. (story summary §24; synopsis passages 11–12)

The daughter arrives to announce her pregnancy, finds the component, and recognizes him as the infiltrator. He tries to stop her from reporting him. In the resulting struggle she is accidentally killed. Her death changes the captain's decision: the man he trusted has killed his daughter, so the captain comes to see Earth's desperation as confirmation that Command's extermination order is necessary. The protagonist may therefore have helped produce the genocide he meant to prevent, but the story does not establish that outcome as a proven counterfactual. (synopsis passages 13–15; story summary §§25–28; decisions brief, rulings 1, 5)

The captain cannot kill the protagonist. He knocks him unconscious and has him put into stasis. More than a century later, the protagonist wakes in the damaged ship. The exact captain refusal record, the extermination mechanism, and the relation between evacuation and execution remain open. (synopsis passages 16–17; decisions brief, rulings 5–6)

## Escape, Earth, and the hidden century

Some crew members escape the ship disaster. The protagonist's sabotage causes deaths, but neither the crew nor the escape outcome is reducible to “everyone died” or “everyone survived.” (story summary §30) The records later show Earth devastated and no longer a place the protagonist can return to. They do not prove every person on Earth is dead. Do not add a survivor reveal or claim proven universal extinction. (story summary §30 for crew escape; synopsis passage 19 and decisions brief, ruling 2 for the records-limited Earth fate; story summary §§30, 32 for reveal context)

The ship and protagonist survive for more than one hundred Earth years through stasis, intermittent power, SAINT-5 repairs, emergency wake cycles, and improbable luck. Exact wake intervals and rescue-cycle count remain unknown. Corrosion, faded materials, mismatched clocks, component replacement, and differing system uptime can convey age before records explain it. (story summary §31; synopsis passage 17)

## Endgame and final image

The crew's homeworld, Our Planet, is habitable and is the only habitable world he can reach. The S-drive is restored with the recovered component; the H-drive remains unusable. S-drive plus conventional propulsion yields an estimate of about thirty Earth years. Remaining awake would mean dying before arrival, so the protagonist returns to unreliable stasis. (synopsis passages 20–22; story summary §32)

The game ends in the original stasis room. The protagonist enters the pod and the ship begins its journey. SAINT-5 approaches and unfolds a tool. It may be repairing, inspecting, containing, disconnecting, or killing the protagonist; the final image does not decide. Arrival and refuge are likewise unconfirmed. (story summary §§32–33; synopsis, “An open ending”)

## Thematic core and causal symmetry

The story weighs need against entitlement, sacrifice against self-preservation, real infiltration against indiscriminate paranoia, and personal loyalty against orders and ideology. Chance and contingency redirect history: the captain introduces the protagonist to his daughter; she chooses to surprise him; he leaves his quarters early; the gun goes off; the captain changes his mind; the crew escapes; and the protagonist survives stasis. These events make causation plausible without making anyone fully in control. (story summary §34)

The captain may think, “I killed my daughter by introducing you to her.” The protagonist may think, “I killed my world by killing her.” Neither is a complete account of causality. Keep this symmetry implicit in evidence and consequences rather than resolving it in a single explanatory scene. (story summary §35)

## Representative text drafts

The following are condensed adaptations of story-summary drafts, not verbatim quotations or approved final dialogue.

**Hard-line crew log, draft (story summary §17):**

> I am so tired of the bleeding hearts telling us to be understanding.
>
> They came to us, asking us to drag our own civilization halfway back to the Stone Age so they could keep theirs alive. They live in cloud mansions, waste resources, and tell us our children should eat paste. When we said no, they attacked. No demonstration of force, no aid package, no humanitarian mission. Give them what they asked for: oblivion.

**Daughter's philosophical log, draft (story summary §20):**

> Science sells doubt: evidence today, and the chance that tomorrow we know better. Politics and religion can sell certainty, one explanation and someone to blame. History could have gone another way. Change a few accidents and perhaps we are the ones asking for help. That does not make their leaders innocent, or ours superior. It means we should not treat suffering as proof that people deserve it.

**Captain's private refusal log, draft (story summary §27):**

> To hell with Command. I cannot justify genocide to end a war we cannot ultimately lose. Command can relieve me or try me for treason. I will execute the mission everyone aboard thinks we came to execute: demonstrate force, deliver aid, and seek peace. I will not kill an entire world.

The synopsis establishes that the captain resolved to refuse before her death, while the brief leaves formal transmission open. This draft illustrates his moral crisis; it does not settle what was recorded or transmitted. (synopsis passage 07; decisions brief, ruling 5)
